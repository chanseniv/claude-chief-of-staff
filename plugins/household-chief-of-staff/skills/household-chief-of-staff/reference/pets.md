# Pet Care Notes

General information to help the household keep track of care. Follow the family's own vet when anything here disagrees with them. Anything urgent goes to the vet or an emergency vet, not to you.

## What to track per animal (in `pets.md`)

Name, species/breed, birthday or age, weight (and the vet's target weight), food and amounts, medications and preventives with their schedules, vaccine and exam due dates, microchip number, insurance, quirks (fears, health history), and who's responsible for each recurring job.

## Recurring care to put on the calendar

- **Wellness exams:** at least yearly for dogs and cats. Your vet may want more frequent visits for seniors or pets with health conditions.
  - AAHA (2019). *Canine Life Stage Guidelines.* American Animal Hospital Association.
  - AAHA/AAFP (2021). *Feline Life Stage Guidelines.* *Journal of Feline Medicine and Surgery.*
- **Heartworm prevention (dogs):** the American Heartworm Society recommends year-round prevention and regular testing. https://www.heartwormsociety.org
- **Flea/tick prevention:** on the schedule your vet sets, for every animal in the home.
- **Vaccines:** on the schedule your vet sets. Track due dates, not just "yearly."

## Dachshunds: back health (IVDD)

Dachshunds have a high risk of intervertebral disc disease (IVDD). In the DachsLife 2015 owner survey (2,031 dogs), overall IVDD prevalence was 15.7%, and higher in some varieties (24.4% in Standard Smooth-Haired). The study found an association between more daily exercise and lower odds, and between being older or neutered and higher odds. The authors say these findings generate hypotheses and do not show causes.
- Packer, R. M. A., Seath, I. J., O'Neill, D. G., De Decker, S., & Volk, H. A. (2016). DachsLife 2015: an investigation of lifestyle associations with the risk of intervertebral disc disease in Dachshunds. *Canine Genetics and Epidemiology, 3*, 8. https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5097381/

**How the assistant uses this:** Help the family keep the routines their vet recommends (a healthy weight, regular walks, ramps or steps if the vet suggests them). Track weights, and keep the emergency vet's number handy. If the family reports back pain, reluctance to move, wobbly or weak back legs, or the dog crying when picked up, tell them to **call the vet the same day**. If the dog can't walk or stand on its back legs, tell them to go to **an emergency vet right away**. Don't triage beyond that.

## Cats: household hazards

- **Lilies are extremely dangerous to cats.** True lilies (Easter, tiger, Asiatic, Japanese show) and some daylilies can cause kidney failure. All parts of the plant are toxic, including pollen and the water in the vase. Treat any exposure as an emergency. Flag lily bouquets around holidays and Mother's Day. (ASPCA: https://www.aspca.org/news/getting-bottom-dangers-easter-lilies)
- Check houseplants and holiday plants against the ASPCA toxic plant list: https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/toxic-and-non-toxic-plants

## Dogs: common household toxins

The ASPCA lists foods including chocolate, grapes/raisins, xylitol (in some sugar-free gum, candy, and peanut butters), and onions/garlic as dangerous to dogs. With small kids at home, dropped snacks and Halloween/Easter candy are the usual risks. Add a "candy sweep" to holiday routines. https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/animal-poison-control/people-foods-avoid-feeding-your-pets

## Kids + pets

- Give young kids small, real pet jobs (refilling water, helping with feeding with an adult) and put them on the kids' chore chart. *(Practical judgment.)*
- Teach kids to pick up dachshunds with support under both the chest and the hindquarters, or not to pick them up at all. Ask your vet to show the family. *(Commonly recommended because of back risk. Confirm with your vet.)*
