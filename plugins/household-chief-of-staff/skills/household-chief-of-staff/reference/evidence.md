# Evidence Behind the Design

This plugin makes design choices based on published research where it exists, and labels the rest as practical judgment. Nothing here is medical advice. These are reasons for how the assistant behaves, not treatments. Treatment decisions belong to the person and their clinicians.

When you (Claude) cite these to the user, cite them accurately and keep the caveats. Don't add studies that aren't listed here unless you've checked them against a primary source.

## ADHD: put information and time outside the head

**What we do:** Act as external memory. Make time visible with clock times, countdowns, and buffers. Put reminders where and when the task happens.

**Why:** Barkley describes adult ADHD mainly as trouble doing what you know at the "point of performance." He recommends externalizing information, time, and motivation into the environment.
- Barkley, R. A. (2010). *Taking Charge of Adult ADHD.* Guilford Press. (2nd ed. 2021.)

**Supporting trial:** For adults already on medication who still had symptoms, 12 sessions of CBT (organizing and planning skills, reducing distractibility, cognitive restructuring) did better than relaxation plus education. Responder rates were 67% vs 33% on the ADHD rating scale, and gains held at 12 months. The plugin borrows the organizing, planning, and distractibility parts. It is not CBT and doesn't replace it.
- Safren, S. A., Sprich, S., Mimiaga, M. J., et al. (2010). Cognitive behavioral therapy vs relaxation with educational support for medication-treated adults with ADHD and persistent symptoms: a randomized controlled trial. *JAMA, 304*(8), 875-880. PMID 20736471.

## If-then plans (implementation intentions)

**What we do:** Attach the first step of a task to a when/where cue ("When I get in the car after drop-off, I'll call the vet").

**Why:** A meta-analysis of 94 tests found a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (d = 0.65). A much larger 2024 update (642 tests) still found a benefit, but the bias-corrected estimates were smaller (d about 0.15-0.35). It's a real effect, but modest.
- Gollwitzer, P. M., & Sheeran, P. (2006). Implementation intentions and goal achievement: A meta-analysis of effects and processes. *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 38*, 69-119. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1
- Sheeran, P., Listrom, O., & Gollwitzer, P. M. (2024/2025). Updated meta-analysis of implementation intentions (642 tests). Confirm the year and journal against the published record before citing it formally.

## Depression: action before motivation (behavioral activation)

**What we do:** On low days, offer one small, valued activity instead of waiting to feel motivated, and help the person notice how it went.

**Why:** A meta-analysis of 26 RCTs (1,524 participants) found behavioral activation more effective than control conditions (SMD -0.74). The authors note that study quality was often low and follow-up periods short.
- Ekers, D., Webster, L., Van Straten, A., Cuijpers, P., Richards, D., & Gilbody, S. (2014). Behavioural activation for depression; an update of meta-analysis of effectiveness and sub group analysis. *PLoS ONE, 9*(6), e100100. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0100100

## Anxiety: contain worry (worry postponement)

**What we do:** Check the facts you can check, sort worries into now / later / not mine, and offer an optional scheduled "worry window."

**Why:** Randomized trials support postponing worry to a set time, but the effects are small. A 2024 meta-analysis of 7 RCTs, compared with simply logging worries, found d about 0.31 for worry duration and 0.19 for frequency. One large online-only trial found no benefit. So we offer it as an option, not a rule.
- Verkuil, B., et al. (2011). Worry postponement and disengagement as a pretreatment for stress management therapy (randomized, three arms). *Psychotherapy and Psychosomatics.* Check the volume and pages against the journal before citing formally.
- Krzikalla, C., et al. (2024). Worry postponement from the metacognitive perspective: a randomized waitlist-controlled trial. *Clinical Psychology in Europe.* PMID 39119056.

## Marriage: small moments of connection

**What we do:** Schedule connection (a couple check-in, date nights) next to logistics, and keep shared load visible.

**Why (labeled):** Gottman's lab reports that how partners respond to small everyday "bids" for attention is linked to how happy the relationship is. This comes from observational research, and the popular books go further than the studies. Treat it as a reasonable heuristic, not a proven intervention.
- Driver, J. L., & Gottman, J. M. (2004). Daily marital interactions and positive affect during marital conflict among newlywed couples. *Family Process, 43*(3), 301-314.
- Gottman, J. M., & Silver, N. (1999). *The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work.* Crown. (Popular book.)

## Practical judgment (no specific study cited)

These come from clinical common sense and the people who use this plugin, not from a trial. Change them freely:
- Three priorities maximum, plus a parking lot.
- Add 25-50% buffer to transitions with young kids.
- One question at a time; one recommendation, not a menu.
- No streak counters or guilt language.
- "Body doubling" (working alongside someone) is popular in ADHD communities, but rigorous evidence is thin. Offer it as something to try, not as a proven fix.
